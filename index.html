The Polychrome Roman Sacrificial Altar

The Roman Altar (Ara) showcased here is a rare surviving example of a painted stone sacrificial altar from ancient Rome, preserved in the collection of the Museo Nazionale Romano (Baths of Diocletian / Palazzo Massimo alle Terme).

Iconography & Construction: Unlike plain marble altars, this piece retains vivid polychrome fresco pigments applied directly to plaster on stone. The front face panel portrays the goddess Ceres (or Abundantia) seated gracefully holding a sheaf of golden wheat ears and a palm branch, symbolizing fertility, agricultural wealth, and divine blessing. At the base rests a sculpted stone gargoyle/mask spout, designed to drain libations or water during sacrificial rituals.

Source Credits: Photo courtesy of www.bellitalie.org | Museo Nazionale Romano - Terme di Diocleziano / Palazzo Massimo, Rome, Italy.

The Wall Paintings of Villa di Termini

The Villa di Termini (often associated with the lavish suburban Villa della Farnesina complex near the Tiber and Termini area) was discovered in 1879 during urban development works around Roma Termini railway station.

Architectural Style & Significance: Dating to the Late Roman Republic / Early Augustan Age (c. 30–20 BCE), these frescoes represent the pinnacle of Second and Third Pompeian Styles. They feature trompe-l'œil architectural frames with cinnabar red columns, delicate garlands, and floating mythological figures set within illusionistic white-ground alcoves.

Archaeological Preservation: The original plaster panel exhibits real historical damages: large lacunae (plaster loss on the left), faded pigment outlines, and structural cracks caused by ground pressure and centuries underground.

Source Credits: Archaeological Heritage of Rome | Museo Nazionale Romano - Palazzo Massimo alle Terme.
